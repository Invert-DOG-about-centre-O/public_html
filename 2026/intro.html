Cambridge University Ethics in Mathematics Society

Ethics for the Working Mathematician

Michaelmas 2026

This course on Ethics in Mathematics aims to give an overview of the sorts of effects mathematicians and mathematical work can have on society, detail the instances where this work can cause harm, and discuss what leads to this work being harmful and what might be done to possibly avert such harm.

This is not a course on ethical frameworks, nor is it a philosophy course. We will not be discussing what is right and wrong, but instead explore the fact that there is right and wrong when doing mathematical work. This is a course to raise fundamental ethical awareness among mathematicians, not to give axioms of ethics or an algorithm for ethical decision-making.

This year, we are again working with the Ethics in Mathematics Project (EiMP) to deliver this course. CUEiMS will be hosting and chairing weekly seminars, given by Dr. Maurice Chiodo; these will consist of an interactive lecture followed by a discussion session.

Practicalities

These lectures will be held at 16:00–18:00 every Tuesday from 13th October to 1st December in MR14 (seminars 1-3) and MR15 (seminars 4-8) in the CMS. The first hour will be a delivered lecture, and the second hour will be a discussion and Q&A session, with a 5 minute break between the two.

Course outline

The eight sessions will be as follows. (Click on titles for further details.)

An introduction to Ethics in Mathematics and why it is important

Mathematicians sit at the heart of technological advancement and industrial progress. Mathematics is a universal tool. It is not neutral, and can be used help, and also to harm. But the world is complicated, and simple notions of `good' and `bad' mask the complexity that happens every time someone does mathematics. This lecture looks at how mathematicians currently perceive what `ethics in mathematics' is, and how through some short interactive problems this perception can be significantly broadened and refined, demonstrating that ethics in mathematics can be taught, and learned. It shows that mathematicians don't need to be scared of ethics and can certainly do it themselves, and moreover that deferring `the ethics' to someone else might not be very effective.

Financial mathematics and modelling

Mathematical modelling is ubiquitous in understanding the way the world works, from finance to physics to disease transmission. Understanding how to develop and use a model, as well as its limitations and the way it interacts with the world, is indispensable in ensuring it is a tool for good, rather than a mechanism for harm. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can avoid causing harm when doing mathematical modelling; harm to others, and harm to themselves. It shows how they can prevent such work tanking financial markets through misapplication, or propagating pathogens or injustices in society though poorly chosen assumptions and use cases, or even landing them in jail through unauthorised actions.

Cryptography, surveillance and privacy

Privacy is a critical part of how society functions, protecting people at the times and places where they are most vulnerable. Mathematicians have always played a central role in the creation and implementation of privacy-preserving, and privacy-infringing, technologies, in many cases determining how much privacy people can have. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can avoid infringing the privacy of others, as this can happen even completely unintentionally. It shows how they might do so deliberately, by designing tools to break strong encryption, or indirectly, by creating systems and platforms which collect massive amounts of personal data, or accidentally, by being careless or sloppy in the way they store and share data.

Fairness and impartiality in algorithms and AI

Mathematicians design the algorithms that run the world, deciding what people read, who they date, and when then can get a loan, often done under the auspice of increasing efficiency and removing human subjectivity. The scale, and speed, at which such algorithms are deployed means that their impact, and potential for harm, can swell far beyond original intentions. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can understand the ways in which their algorithms rank, predict, suggest, and rate things for and about people and society, and how this might lead to harm. It shows how they can see the subjectivity of their work as a strength when interpreted correctly, as well as how to avoid any large-scale runaway behaviour and subsequent harm.

Regulation, accountability, and the law

Mathematical work in industry is now very close to its tangible impact, and such outputs are now extremely quick and easy to implement, with impacts that are amplified by modern computational processes. But with such great power comes great responsibility, and so various aspects of criminal, civil, and moral accountability can quickly come in to play for those carrying out such work. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can learn just how close they now sit to the applications of their work, and associated accountability mechanisms they may face even in circumstances where they were `just following orders'. It shows how there are things mathematicians are, and aren't, legally (or morally) allowed to do, and how they can change course to avoid consequences in the courts of justice and of public opinion for falling foul of such expectations.

Understanding the behaviour of the mathematical community

Mathematicians, just like every other academic field, form a community, maintaining and handing down their own conventions, common beliefs, and schools of thought through the generations. This `mathematical' approach is extremely effective when working on abstract mathematics problems, but doesn't always work as well when considering broader industrial problems, even ones that involve mathematics. This lecture looks at how mathematicians are affected by the way they are trained and taught, and how the habits, norms, and perspectives they pick up can go on to affect and inhibit the way they carry out mathematical work, potentially leading to harm. It shows how they can identify and bridge any shortcomings in their skill sets, and how to release the notion that mathematics is `pure and neutral' in order to produce good solutions to industrial or social problems.

Psychology 101: How to survive as a mathematician at work

Mathematicians eventually form part of the workforce, facing the same workplace interactions, issues, conflicts and pressures that arise in all other professions, and needing to know how to deal with these. Yet their focused and dedicated nature, enhanced by the training they receive, might make it easy to overlook instances of others trying to exploit or manipulate them into producing harmful work. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can be susceptible to factors such as false choice, workplace pressure, intentional manipulation, and social conformity. It shows how they can start to identify such situations, and provides insight and empowerment to protect themselves, and their work, against these harmful influences.

Looking into the future, what more can mathematicians do?

Mathematicians need an acute awareness of the ethical issues they may face during their career, but this is only the first in a sequence of steps required to do mathematics responsibly. Beyond knowledge, actions and changes in behaviour are necessary in order to turn safety and ethics from a theoretical concept to lived practice, and such actions are not always immediately obvious. This lecture looks at how mathematicians can take this next step, from increasing their level of ethical engagement, to looking past oaths and codes as `quick-fixes', to operationalising responsible development, and to seeing how to factor in other points of view and put their own thoughts into writing. It shows how they can `be the change' and transition from thinking about ethics, to doing ethics, leading them out from the insights of the course and into more proactive roles as responsible mathematicians.

Further reading

There are very few written resources relating to ethics in mathematics; as an area of study and understanding it is remarkably new. In terms of textbooks, there is nothing. The closest one can conceivably come is the following book, which is a popular science book (thus not written for a mathematical audience):

  • C. O’Neill, Weapons of Math Destruction, Penguin (2016). 272 pages.

However, there is now a decent collection of articles and other publications on Ethics in Mathematics one can read. For convenience these are below divided into four sections. It would be wise, and easy, to read through the short articles in sections 1 and 2 above. These cover what Ethics in Mathematics is, and how one can go about learning it. The book by O’Neill is a fine, if somewhat superficial and mathematically-lacking, broad introduction to where some ethical issues in mathematics lie. Section 3 is much more involved, and would bring you up to speed on the ‘state of the art’ of Ethics in Mathematics. Section 4 then covers the complexities present in this rather nascent field of research and study.

None of these are required reading for the course, though may be of interest and some use in helping you to put Ethics in Mathematics into some more context.
